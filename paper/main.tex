\title{Cloud Optical Thickness for Satellite-Based Solar Irradiance Estimation and Short-Term Forecasting}
\author{}\date{}
\begin{document}
\begin{center}\LARGE\bfseries
Cloud Optical Thickness for Satellite-Based Solar Irradiance Estimation and Short-Term Forecasting
\end{center}
\begin{abstract}
Cloud optical thickness (COT) describes cloud attenuation of solar radiation, but its added value for irradiance estimation is uncertain when multispectral satellite imagery is already available. We develop a COT-assisted approach that combines local optical-thickness fields retrieved from FY-4B/AGRI imagery with spectral and solar-geometric information to estimate 15-minute mean global horizontal irradiance (GHI). The approach is evaluated against models without COT at three stations in Hubei and two in Hunan, with models trained independently in each region. On the Hubei validation samples, adding COT reduces GHI root mean square error from 74.41 to 68.63 W\,m$^{-2}$ (7.76\%). The largest reduction, 35.51\%, occurs in the thick-cloud subset covered by the satellite reference product and is accompanied by lower positive irradiance bias; improvements under thin and moderately thick clouds are less certain. Applying the irradiance models to predicted imagery extends the approach to 15--240 min forecasting, where COT reduces regional validation RMSE by 4.90\% in Hubei and 1.70\% in Hunan. Controlled input replacements further show that predicted COT contains useful cloud-evolution information, while GHI forecasts remain sensitive to errors in that representation. These results support explicit COT as a useful complement to multispectral imagery for irradiance estimation and short-term forecasting, with benefits that vary across cloud conditions and regions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Clouds modulate the solar radiation reaching the surface, and changes in their location and optical thickness can produce substantial variability at a ground station. Geostationary multispectral imagery provides frequent observations of these changes and underpins satellite estimates of surface solar irradiance \cite{huang2019}. Cloud optical thickness (COT) describes extinction through a cloud column. Its retrieval from reflected solar radiation has a physical basis, although the inverse problem can be ambiguous for optically thin water clouds \cite{nakajima1990}. These properties make COT a potentially useful representation for irradiance estimation.

Satellite irradiance methods have long represented cloud attenuation explicitly. Early cloud-index methods relate observed reflectance to clear-surface and cloudy references \cite{cano1986}; Heliosat-2 combines calibrated satellite observations with atmospheric-optics modelling \cite{rigollier2004}. These indices are distinct from COT. Physical approaches instead use retrieved cloud properties in radiative-transfer calculations or their approximations. Takenaka et al. developed a neural-network approximation of radiative transfer for surface radiation estimation \cite{takenaka2011}, while Heliosat-4 separates clear-sky irradiance and cloud extinction using lookup tables and satellite cloud properties \cite{qu2017}. These studies establish the role of cloud optical properties in radiation estimation; they do not determine how much an explicit COT input adds to a model that already receives multispectral imagery.

Data-driven retrievals learn spectral, spatial, and temporal relationships with surface irradiance. Chen et al. examined spectral selection for global and direct irradiance estimation \cite{chen2023}. STARNet incorporates neighbouring spatial and temporal information from FY-4A \cite{song2025}, and Schuurman and Meyer combined satellite-product emulation with ground-observation fine-tuning for Meteosat irradiance retrieval \cite{schuurman2025}. Cloud-property information can also be learned separately: Xia et al. retrieved nighttime COT from FY-4B for single-layer non-precipitating clouds \cite{xia2025}. In a hybrid irradiance approach, Liao and Coimbra compared CNNs with and without SCOPE-derived features and extended the approach to short-term forecasting \cite{liao2024}. Together, these approaches motivate testing a cloud-supervised representation alongside the original imagery. In our case, COT is derived from those same observations; it introduces cloud-property supervision without adding an independent measurement.

The contribution of this representation may depend on cloud conditions. Cloud optical properties and aerosols affect radiation errors \cite{pap2024}, while satellite viewing geometry and the displacement between a cloud and its surface shadow complicate comparisons with station measurements \cite{wiltink2025}. Recent FY-4B forecasting work also employs weather-dependent correction of satellite-to-station irradiance differences \cite{shen2026fy4b}. Overall error alone therefore gives an incomplete description of performance. We compare the effect of COT within fixed optical-thickness ranges and examine continuous cloud-field and irradiance evolution. These comparisons link an aggregate gain to the cloud conditions and irradiance variations in which it occurs.

Forecasting introduces uncertainty in the future cloud field. Sirch et al. extrapolated cloud optical thickness for direct-normal irradiance nowcasting \cite{sirch2017}; Miller et al. combined cloud advection, cloud properties, and shadow geometry in solar irradiance forecasts \cite{miller2018}. Learned approaches predict different intermediate quantities: IrradianceNet forecasts satellite-derived irradiance fields \cite{nielsen2021}, Chen et al. use a chain of deep-learning models for satellite-based intraday forecasting \cite{chen2024}, and Cloud-FNO predicts cloud physical properties \cite{zhang2026}. These approaches demonstrate several ways to connect cloud evolution and radiation, with different forecast targets and reference data.

In our image-extrapolation chain, the retrieval and irradiance models receive predicted imagery. Errors in cloud position, boundaries, or growth and decay can reach GHI both through the image input and through retrieved COT. Comparisons of satellite nowcasting methods show that numerical accuracy, spatial appearance, and performance under different sky conditions need not favour the same model \cite{cui2024}. We therefore assess the effect of image prediction through paired input substitutions and aligned cloud and irradiance trajectories, alongside overall error statistics, to examine complementary aspects of forecast performance \cite{yang2020}.

We address three questions: (1) how much does local COT improve GHI estimation beyond AGRI imagery and solar geometry; (2) under which optical-thickness conditions and cloud events does the improvement occur; and (3) how much of that benefit remains in 15--240 min forecasts, and how is it constrained by cloud-image extrapolation errors? Hubei provides the primary station comparisons, while independently trained Hunan models provide complementary regional evidence. The work focuses on the contribution of cloud-property representation and its error propagation; developing an improved image-extrapolation model is a subsequent research question.

\section{Study area and data}
\subsection{Regions, satellite observations, and cloud reference}
The primary region contains Niushou, Wangzhai, and Mizhuang in Hubei. Sili and Zhujia in Hunan form the complementary region. Models and normalization are fitted independently within each region. Regional results are reported separately because station observations have different native temporal sampling.

The AGRI inputs comprise thirteen channels, C01--C06 and C09--C15, with region-specific geolocation. The same station indices register the imagery, geometry, and COT fields. The COT retriever reads a $16\times16$ patch; the irradiance model selects a station-centred $3\times3$ neighbourhood from that patch. 

The cloud reference consists of archived FY-4B/AGRI cloud physical property (CPP) retrievals, including dimensionless COT and cloud phase, registered to the regional AGRI grid by their latitude and longitude coordinates. Supervised COT pixels require finite reference values in $[0,100]$ and daytime solar zenith angles below $80^\circ$; valid zero-COT values are retained. Hunan additionally requires valid AGRI inputs and a valid clear/water/ice phase category, whereas Hubei uses the COT validity mask independently of phase validity. Missing reference values are excluded from COT supervision and reference-based stratification, but CPP availability does not restrict the main GHI comparison. CPP is a satellite retrieval reference, not an independent ground truth; the historical production-version limitations are documented in the supplementary provenance description.

\subsection{Irradiance targets and solar geometry}
Let $\tau$ denote the end of a 15-minute target interval. The target is the interval-mean GHI, $\bar G_{s,\tau}$, at station $s$. Hunan five-minute interval-end records are aggregated as
\begin{equation}
\bar G_{s,\tau}=\frac13\sum_{j=0}^{2}G^{(5)}_{s,\tau-5j\,\mathrm{min}}.
\label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Hubei labels are formed from the valid one-minute bins in the same right-closed interval; incomplete windows are excluded. The clear-sky irradiance $\bar G^{cs}_{s,\tau}$ is averaged over that interval using the Ineichen--Perez model \cite{ineichen,pvlibclear}. The learning target and reconstruction are
\begin{equation}
k_{s,\tau}=\frac{\bar G_{s,\tau}}{\bar G^{cs}_{s,\tau}},\qquad
\widehat G_{s,\tau}=\widehat k_{s,\tau}\bar G^{cs}_{s,\tau}.
\label{eq:kt}
\end{equation}
The clear-sky index is not capped at one. GHI observations serve as labels, not model inputs. Solar geometry is computed from UTC, latitude, and longitude and registered with each image. The cosine of solar zenith and the cosine of the solar--viewing relative azimuth provide the geometric inputs; the retriever also receives a daytime indicator.

\subsection{Temporal sampling}
Training, validation, and final evaluation dates are separated at the date-block level to reduce dependence between fitting and evaluation samples \cite{roberts2017}. In both regions, sufficiently covered months reserve separate consecutive two-day blocks for validation and testing, with one-day buffers on each side; months with fewer than twenty eligible days contribute only training data. All frames of a three-frame GHI example or an eight-history/sixteen-future forecasting example must remain in the same partition. Normalization is fitted on training data alone. The fixed imagery partitions contain 34,854/3,171 training/validation sequences in Hubei and 35,320/3,140 in Hunan. These are image-sequence counts, distinct from the station GHI counts in Table~\ref{tab:data}. Targets and clear-sky quantities are identical within each paired GHI comparison. The results reported here use validation data; the reserved test partitions have not been evaluated.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Supervised COT retrieval}
For imagery $A_\tau$ and geometry $g_\tau$, the retriever produces a nonnegative log-COT field,
\begin{equation}
L_\tau=R_\theta(A_\tau,g_\tau),\qquad C_\tau=\exp(L_\tau)-1.
\label{eq:retrieval}
\end{equation}
The retriever follows the U-Net encoder--decoder architecture \cite{unet2015}, with two downsampling stages, feature widths 32, 64, and 128 in Hunan and 16, 32, and 64 in Hubei, and skip-connected upsampling to the original $16\times16$ grid. Convolutional blocks use batch normalization and SiLU; the final softplus produces nonnegative $L_\tau$. It is trained against $\log(1+C^{CPP})$ with a masked Huber objective \cite{huber1964},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_R=\frac{\sum_{i,p}m_{i,p}\rho_{0.1}\!\left(L_{i,p}-\log(1+C^{CPP}_{i,p})\right)}{\sum_{i,p}m_{i,p}},
\label{eq:retrieval-loss}
\end{equation}
where $m$ identifies valid common input and reference pixels. Valid zero-COT references are retained. Training normalization is retained at inference; inference validity uses available inputs and geometry, without requiring a future CPP reference. Reference-quality criteria apply to supervised training and reference-based evaluation. The retrieval-model parameters remain fixed during irradiance-model training and forecasting.

\subsection{Irradiance estimation using local COT fields}
Let $\mathcal P_s$ select the station-centred $3\times3$ field. AGRI channels and log-COT are standardized with training statistics:
\begin{equation}
X_{s,\tau,c}=\frac{\mathcal P_sA_{\tau,c}-\mu_c}{\sigma_c},\qquad
Z_{s,\tau}=\frac{\mathcal P_sL_\tau-\mu_L}{\sigma_L}.
\label{eq:standardization}
\end{equation}
The model receives all nine COT values in their original spatial arrangement, preserving local variation and differences in magnitude between samples. The two irradiance models are
\begin{equation}
\widehat k^A_{s,\tau}=H_A(X_{s,\tau-30:\tau},g_{s,\tau-30:\tau}),\qquad
\widehat k^{AC}_{s,\tau}=H_{AC}(X_{s,\tau-30:\tau},g_{s,\tau-30:\tau},Z_{s,\tau-30:\tau}).
\label{eq:heads}
\end{equation}
The primary estimator uses the ordered three-frame sequence at $\tau-30$, $\tau-15$ and $\tau$ minutes. At each time, the 13 AGRI channels, two geometric channels and standardized log-COT are registered on the same station-centred $3\times3$ grid. A neutral extra slot gives the implemented tensor shape $[3,17,3,3]$. A temporal convolution maps 17 channels to 32 with a $3\times3\times3$ kernel; a $3\times3$ spatial convolution retains 32 channels, followed by ordered flattening and a $288\to64\to1$ readout. SiLU activations precede the final softplus. Both paired models have 42,529 trainable parameters and receive identical initialization; only the standardized COT slot is set to zero for the baseline. The frame order and local spatial positions are retained, without imposing a cloud-motion trajectory. Original single-frame designs are described in Supplementary material S4.

The models minimize physical GHI mean squared error, scaled for numerical convenience:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_H=\frac1N\sum_i\left[\frac{\widehat k_i\bar G^{cs}_i-\bar G_i}{1000\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}}\right]^2.
\label{eq:ghi-loss}
\end{equation}
Paired training uses the same examples, random seeds, sample order, optimization budget, and selection rule. Table~\ref{tab:training} specifies the final architecture and settings for each evaluated configuration.

The COT retrievers use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, gradient-norm clipping at 5, and a maximum of 50 epochs. Training stops after eight epochs without improvement in validation masked log-COT Huber loss; the lowest-loss checkpoint is retained. The GHI estimators use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$ for 30 epochs, cosine decay to $10^{-5}$, and gradient-norm clipping at 5. Each station and random seed selects the checkpoint with the lowest validation GHI RMSE. AGRI and log-COT standardization is fixed from training data, while the two solar-geometric inputs retain their physical values. Initial parameters, sample orders, optimizer settings, and update counts are identical within each COT comparison. The retriever and image-predictor parameters remain fixed when evaluating GHI forecasts.

\subsection{Short-term forecasting}
With initialization $t$ defined by the last historical image, a fixed image predictor $S_\psi$ generates
\begin{equation}
\widehat A_{t+k\Delta}=S_\psi(A_{t-7\Delta:t},g)_{k},\qquad
\widehat C_{t+k\Delta}=\exp R_\theta(\widehat A_{t+k\Delta},g_{t+k\Delta})-1,
\label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}
for $k=1,\ldots,16$ and $\Delta=15$ min. SimVP serves as the fixed baseline \cite{simvp,simvpv2}. For the regional forecasting comparisons, the diffusion-based image predictor DyDiff is trained independently in Hubei and Hunan. Each model is trained for 150 epochs, and its exponential-moving-average weights are selected by validation forecast RMSE. GHI evaluation uses one forecast member generated with 25 sampling steps. Both image predictors use the same downstream comparisons. Future solar geometry is deterministic. Future observed imagery and CPP are excluded from forecasting inputs.

The regional DyDiff predictors share a factorized transformer architecture with patch size 8, width 384, twelve layers, six attention heads, and an MLP expansion ratio of 4 (65,794,266 trainable parameters). They are independently trained for 150 epochs using a 1,000-step mixed-velocity diffusion objective, AdamW with initial learning rate $10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$, two warm-up epochs, a minimum learning rate of $10^{-6}$, and gradient-norm clipping at 1. Exponential moving average weights use an update-dependent warm-up and a maximum decay of 0.999. Model selection uses fixed-seed, single-member forecast RMSE over the full validation set in the training-normalized image space; GHI evaluation uses 25-step single-member sampling. Image normalization is fitted separately on each region's training partition.

We apply the two irradiance models trained on observed imagery to forecast imagery, keeping their parameters fixed, to determine whether the benefit of including COT persists in forecasting. For the three-frame configuration, +15 min includes two known historical frames and +30 min includes one; results for leads $\geq45$ min separately assess inputs consisting entirely of forecast imagery.



\section{Experimental design}
\subsection{Evaluation of the contribution of COT}
Each comparison uses identical station, initialization where applicable, target time, GHI label, and valid daytime mask. Results are computed for each station and random seed before equal-weight averaging within region. We report physical RMSE, MAE, bias, and the relative RMSE reduction
\begin{equation}
\eta=100\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_{A}-\mathrm{RMSE}_{AC}}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{A}}.
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
Uncertainty is evaluated using 5,000 paired bootstrap draws of target-date blocks defined in Beijing time, retaining overlapping forecast records within the same resampled block. This follows the principle of resampling groups rather than treating correlated records as independent observations \cite{field2007}. The final tables identify the evaluation partition and date count. Additional image features with matched irradiance-model capacity and features extracted from the fixed COT retrieval model are evaluated to distinguish the effects of model capacity and cloud-property supervision.

\subsection{Cloud conditions and continuous events}
GHI errors are stratified by station-centre CPP reference COT using fixed near-zero, thin, moderate, and thick bins: $[0,0.1)$, $[0.1,5)$, $[5,30)$, and $[30,100]$. All models are compared on the same samples with valid CPP references within each bin; the numbers of samples and dates are reported. The overall GHI evaluation also includes samples without CPP observations. Solar-geometry and regional stratification test whether an apparent cloud-thickness pattern reflects other conditions.

Continuous cases combine observed AGRI composites, retrieved and reference COT, and interval-mean GHI. At Niushou, the first station in the fixed study order, candidate validation dates must contain at least twelve of the seventeen 15-minute targets between 10:30 and 14:30 BJT, a station-pixel retrieved COT peak of at least 30, and a COT range of at least 5. We select the earliest eligible date in each of the improving and non-improving RMSE groups. This conditional selection illustrates contrasting responses without selecting the largest changes; the cases do not estimate their frequency. The retrieved-COT condition is separate from the CPP-based thickness strata.

\subsection{Effects of cloud-image prediction errors on COT and GHI}
We keep the retrieval model and the irradiance model with COT unchanged and evaluate all input combinations on the same samples. Let $A^o$ and $A^f$ denote observed and forecast imagery, and let $C^o=R_C(A^o,g)$ and $C^f=R_C(A^f,g)$ denote the corresponding physical COT fields from the same retriever. The four input combinations $(A^o,C^o)$, $(A^f,C^o)$, $(A^o,C^f)$, and $(A^f,C^f)$ isolate sensitivity to the image and COT input routes. The first three use future observations and serve only as offline mechanism experiments.

All comparisons use the same stations, forecast initialization times, target intervals, observations, valid samples, and sample weights. The preprocessing, including log-COT standardization, is identical for every input combination. In the three-frame configuration, known historical positions remain identical; only future positions are replaced. For $a,b\in\{0,1\}$, define $E_{ab}$ as mean squared GHI error, with 0 denoting observed-derived inputs and 1 forecast-derived inputs. Then
\begin{equation}
E_{11}-E_{00}=\underbrace{(E_{10}-E_{00})}_{D_A}
+\underbrace{(E_{01}-E_{00})}_{D_C}
+\underbrace{(E_{11}-E_{10}-E_{01}+E_{00})}_{I}.
\label{eq:propagation}
\end{equation}
The interaction $I$ allows the GHI response to joint input errors to differ from the sum of separate replacements. This is an exact decomposition of prediction-error sensitivity, not a decomposition into independent physical causes. All terms are evaluated as MSE on the same samples. COT trajectory errors, cloud-boundary displacement, and timing of station irradiance changes provide physical interpretation of the replacement contrasts.

To determine whether image prediction errors reduce the improvement from COT, we also evaluate the irradiance model without COT on observed and forecast imagery using the same samples. Let its MSEs be $B_o$ and $B_f$. The COT advantages are $V_o=B_o-E_{00}$ and $V_f=B_f-E_{11}$, so
\begin{equation}
V_o-V_f=(E_{11}-E_{00})-(B_f-B_o).
\label{eq:value-loss}
\end{equation}
A positive value indicates that replacing observed imagery with forecast imagery reduces the MSE advantage of the model with COT; a negative value indicates that this advantage increases. The sign is evaluated before interpreting any reduction in forecasting benefit.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Evaluation of retrieved COT}
On 67,187 valid $16\times16$ CPP-reference pixels from 264 validation frames and 16 target dates, the Hubei retriever has COT RMSE 10.829, MAE 6.054, and bias $+1.185$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrieval}). These dimensionless measures quantify agreement with the CPP retrieval reference, rather than independent cloud truth. GHI evaluation includes all valid station labels, including dates without CPP; the retrieval-reference and station-GHI sample counts therefore differ.

\subsection{Effect of COT on irradiance estimation}
Adding COT to the observed three-frame Hubei inputs reduces station- and seed-averaged GHI RMSE from 74.406 to 68.629 W\,m$^{-2}$, a 7.76\% reduction (Fig.~\ref{fig:gain}). The paired difference is $-5.776$ W\,m$^{-2}$ (95\% target-date-block interval: $[-9.650,-2.313]$). A consecutive-two-day-block sensitivity analysis gives an interval of $[-10.419,-1.698]$ W\,m$^{-2}$. All nine station--random-seed pairs improve. The comparison contains 1,905 common station--time records across 20 validation dates; no forecast imagery enters these estimates.

Niushou improves from 69.968 to 64.451 W\,m$^{-2}$ (7.88\%), Wangzhai from 67.452 to 59.215 W\,m$^{-2}$ (12.21\%), and Mizhuang from 85.797 to 82.222 W\,m$^{-2}$ (4.17\%). Mizhuang's date-block interval includes zero, so improvement is not resolved equally at every station. The trainable head capacity, initial weights, example order, optimizer and number of updates are matched within each pair. Thus, additional trainable GHI capacity alone does not explain this paired difference, while the comparison does not isolate COT from every alternative cloud-supervised representation.

The Hubei three-frame data contain 20,035 training records. Target labels are retained from the fixed candidate pool when all three observed frames are available in the same partition. For complementary Hunan evidence, the fixed three-frame models evaluated on the 63,480 forecast-associated validation records give 90.318 W\,m$^{-2}$ without COT and 89.155 W\,m$^{-2}$ with COT (1.29\%); its paired interval includes zero ($[-3.345,0.958]$ W\,m$^{-2}$). These overlapping records use the forecast-comparison weighting and are not 63,480 independent station observations. \subsection{Dependence on optical thickness and cloud conditions}
The reference-covered Hubei subsets show RMSE reductions of 4.20\%, 6.09\%, 6.87\%, 35.51\% for near-zero, thin, moderate and thick COT, respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:thickness}). These subsets contain 226, 61, 185 and 140 station--time records across 9, 4, 6 and 6 target dates. Thick-cloud RMSE decreases from 51.521 to 33.224 W\,m$^{-2}$; its relative reduction has a 95\% interval of $[29.92,44.43]$\%. Thick-cloud mean bias decreases from $+22.408$ to $+0.635$ W\,m$^{-2}$, consistent with correcting excessive irradiance under strong optical attenuation.

The thin- and moderate-cloud reduction intervals include zero. Near-zero RMSE decreases, but its positive bias increases from $+2.921$ to $+9.830$ W\,m$^{-2}$; moderate-cloud bias changes from $-8.975$ to $-19.541$ W\,m$^{-2}$. COT therefore improves error magnitude without consistently improving signed bias in every condition. The 612 reference-covered records are a subset of the 1,905 overall GHI records; missing CPP is not interpreted as clear sky. Limited date coverage restricts generalization of the large thick-cloud reduction.

\subsection{Cloud events and irradiance response}
The two Niushou cases show that COT changes the irradiance response in both directions, rather than imposing a uniform attenuation (Fig.~\ref{fig:events}). On 26 September 2025, station-pixel retrieved COT ranges from 33.5 to 48.2 while observed GHI remains between 50.4 and 107.1 W\,m$^{-2}$. Adding COT raises underestimated late-morning values and reduces early-afternoon overestimation. Averaging the three seed-specific RMSEs gives 27.8 W\,m$^{-2}$ without COT and 19.3 W\,m$^{-2}$ with COT on fifteen common intervals. Retrieved COT is generally lower than the available CPP reference, yet the added representation still improves GHI in this case.

On 27 September, retrieved COT ranges from 18.4 to 31.7 and is generally higher than the CPP reference. At 14:00, observed interval-mean GHI rises to 484.2 W\,m$^{-2}$ while both models remain much lower. The COT-assisted model gives the lower prediction and increases RMSE from 92.7 to 100.9 W\,m$^{-2}$ over sixteen common intervals. The juxtaposition of the cloud field, station COT and GHI shows that a local thickness representation can improve a low-irradiance period but does not guarantee that rapid irradiance changes are captured. These adjacent-date examples are not independent repetitions and do not isolate retrieval error from spatial or temporal representativeness.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Contribution of COT to irradiance estimation}
COT describes optical attenuation by clouds and provides a physically interpretable input to the irradiance model. Training the retrieval model against cloud products may help the irradiance model use cloud information that is implicit in the spectral inputs. Comparisons with additional image features and retrieval-model features assess how much of the improvement is associated with increased model capacity or cloud-property supervision. The original-geometry comparisons in Supplementary material S4 suggest a contribution from cloud-product supervision and motivate evaluating thickness-dependent bias. Their absolute gains do not establish the corresponding effects for a model using different solar geometry. The Hubei three-frame result provides a matched-head-capacity demonstration of a COT-related GHI improvement. The larger thick-cloud correction is associated with lower positive irradiance bias; the smaller Hunan point reduction is not resolved from zero. Cloud-supervised representation is therefore useful under some evaluated conditions, rather than uniformly beneficial across regions and cloud regimes.

Thickness-dependent gains should be interpreted through both attenuation and irradiance variability. Radiative-transfer-based estimation links optical-property errors to surface fluxes, while unresolved broken clouds complicate agreement with point observations \cite{takenaka2011}. Cloud edges, diffuse radiation, and changes during a 15-minute interval further complicate the mapping to station GHI. Cloud-shadow displacement and viewing parallax can also change which cloud pixels are relevant to a station \cite{miller2018,wiltink2025}. The continuous cases illustrate contrasting irradiance responses to the added representation, but do not separate these physical processes.

\subsection{Cloud-image extrapolation as a constraint on forecasting benefit}
The regional forecast results extend the evidence for COT from observed-image estimation to predicted-image inputs: overall RMSE decreases by 4.90\% in Hubei and 1.70\% in Hunan without adapting the irradiance models to forecasts. These are regional validation comparisons, not evidence of uniform gains across stations or cloud conditions. The Hubei forecast reduction is numerically smaller than the 7.76\% observed-input reduction, but their samples and temporal weighting differ. That difference alone cannot quantify the effect of image extrapolation on the COT benefit.

Errors in predicted cloud images can affect GHI directly through the spectral and spatial inputs and indirectly through the COT retrieved from those images. Errors in cloud position can change the location and timing of attenuation at the station. Spatial smoothing can reduce local COT contrasts, while errors in cloud growth and dissipation can suppress or delay changes in optical thickness. Prior nowcasting studies distinguish spatial appearance and numerical errors, and identify difficulties in representing cloud evolution \cite{chen2024,cui2024}. If the COT-related improvement is smaller with forecast imagery, these processes could help explain the reduction.

The input-replacement results distinguish two questions: whether COT helps relative to an imagery baseline, and how accurate the COT-assisted model could be with better COT inputs. On the same Hunan records and with the same three-frame models, observed imagery gives 90.318 W\,m$^{-2}$ without COT and 89.155 W\,m$^{-2}$ with COT, a 1.29\% reduction whose paired interval includes zero ($[-3.345,0.958]$ W\,m$^{-2}$). This point reduction is smaller than the 1.74\% obtained with DyDiff imagery. Hence, these comparisons do not show that extrapolation reduces the relative benefit of COT. Nevertheless, the large improvement after replacing forecast-derived COT with observed-derived COT indicates a sensitivity to errors entering through this input. It does not separate cloud-image prediction error from retrieval behaviour on predicted images or the irradiance model's response to the substituted inputs. The single-image and three-frame percentage gains also involve different samples and representations and cannot be interpreted as an error budget.

Improving the prediction of cloud position, boundaries, and optical-thickness evolution is therefore a direction for further research. Holding the retrieval and irradiance models fixed would allow future work to test whether more accurate cloud-image predictions lead to further reductions in GHI forecast error.

\subsection{Applicability and limitations}
The applicability of the results depends on regional sampling, reference-product uncertainty, and the number of independent weather periods evaluated. CPP-based error measures assess agreement with a retrieval reference, whereas GHI performance is assessed against station observations. Ground-reference quality also matters: a recent FY-4B validation study showed that radiometer quality can materially affect the assessment of satellite irradiance products \cite{chen2025fy4b}. \section{Conclusions}
This study evaluates explicit COT as a local representation for satellite GHI estimation and follows its effect into short-term forecasting. In Hubei, COT reduces observed three-frame GHI RMSE by 7.76\%, with point improvements at all three stations. The reference-covered thick-cloud subset shows a 35.51\% reduction and substantially lower positive bias, while thin- and moderate-cloud intervals include zero. The complementary Hunan observed-input comparison gives a smaller 1.29\% point reduction with an interval crossing zero. With independently trained regional DyDiff predictors and fixed observed-trained three-frame irradiance models, 15--240 min validation RMSE decreases by 4.90\% in Hubei and 1.70\% in Hunan. At +45--240 min, when all three frames are predicted, the point reductions are 4.73\% and 1.71\%, respectively; the Hubei subset interval includes zero. These results extend the observed-input evidence to an overall forecasting benefit in both regions. Fixed-model COT replacements indicate both useful predicted evolution and remaining sensitivity to COT input errors; they do not identify a unique physical cause or demonstrate a reduction of relative COT benefit due to image extrapolation. The resulting evidence provides a basis for improving irradiance-relevant cloud evolution in future image-extrapolation models.

\subsection*{S4. Estimation comparisons under the original solar-geometry preprocessing}
These comparisons use stored models fitted with the original solar-geometry preprocessing. The Hubei CPP supervision is correctly matched in UTC, but both its retrieval inputs and GHI geometry retain that original preprocessing. The Hunan single-image comparison also retains its original geometry and fixed selected weights, with a common station-day quality exclusion. These validation estimates are reproducible configuration-specific results, not the primary verified-geometry estimation scores. The thickness-stratified Hubei results have the same provenance as its overall comparison.

Under the original geometry, adding COT lowers single-image GHI errors in both regional validation sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:legacy_gain}). In Hubei, the station- and seed-averaged RMSE decreases from 77.285 to 69.142 W\,m$^{-2}$, a reduction of 10.54\%. The paired difference is $-8.143$ W\,m$^{-2}$ (95\% two-day-block interval: $[-14.999,-3.361]$). MAE decreases from 56.616 to 46.723 W\,m$^{-2}$, and positive bias decreases from 24.799 to 13.236 W\,m$^{-2}$. The evaluation contains 2,195 station--time records in ten two-day blocks, with three random initializations. All three station means improve: Niushou, 76.851 to 63.978; Wangzhai, 70.539 to 63.137; and Mizhuang, 84.466 to 80.312 W\,m$^{-2}$.

The complementary Hunan single-image comparison contains 4,440 station--time records across 51 target dates. RMSE decreases from 108.181 to 103.995 W\,m$^{-2}$ (3.87\%), with a paired difference of $-4.187$ W\,m$^{-2}$ and a 95\% date-block interval of $[-6.652,-1.855]$. Sili improves from 103.558 to 99.877 W\,m$^{-2}$ and Zhujia from 112.805 to 108.113 W\,m$^{-2}$. The comparison is conditional on these preprocessing configurations; it does not isolate the effect of correcting solar geometry.

The Hunan representation controls provide context for this improvement (Table~\ref{tab:legacy_controls}). Additional image features yield an RMSE of 108.014 W\,m$^{-2}$, close to the baseline, whereas retrieval-model features yield 103.456 W\,m$^{-2}$, comparable to explicit COT. Thus, cloud-supervised features can be useful without expressing that information solely as optical thickness. The result supports COT as a useful representation in this setting, but does not establish that explicit thickness is uniquely responsible for the entire gain. In the Hubei cloud-probability comparison, continuous cloud probability alone yields 74.053 W\,m$^{-2}$; combining probability with COT yields 68.815 W\,m$^{-2}$. Its additional reduction relative to COT alone is 0.47\%, with a 95\% interval of $[-1.23,2.62]$\%, providing no clear overall incremental benefit.

The Hubei reference-covered subsets show a marked dependence on optical thickness (Fig.~\ref{fig:legacy_thickness}). For near-zero, thin, moderate, and thick reference COT, RMSE decreases from 88.704 to 85.145, from 116.483 to 106.130, from 77.587 to 75.281, and from 57.139 to 34.527 W\,m$^{-2}$, respectively. The corresponding reductions are 4.01\%, 8.89\%, 2.97\%, and 39.57\%. The thick-cloud subset contains 173 station--time records in four two-day blocks; its 95\% reduction interval is $[28.04,50.05]$\%. Near-zero, thin, and moderate subsets contain 268, 71, and 211 records across six, three, and five blocks, respectively, and their reduction intervals include zero. The overall GHI result includes records without CPP and therefore is not a weighted average of these four subsets.

In the thick-cloud subset, mean bias decreases from $+36.097$ to $+6.093$ W\,m$^{-2}$, consistent with correcting excessive transmitted irradiance under strong attenuation. This does not imply a universal downward correction: moderate-cloud bias changes from $+14.490$ to $-11.023$ W\,m$^{-2}$, while near-zero bias increases from $+6.218$ to $+14.284$ W\,m$^{-2}$. At Mizhuang, near-zero-COT RMSE increases from 114.775 to 118.948 W\,m$^{-2}$. The strongest aggregate improvement therefore coexists with adverse responses in particular conditions and stations. The non-monotonic reductions and limited reference coverage preclude a general claim that benefit increases continuously with thickness.

The forecast-trained comparison separates historical-COT information from the conditional contribution of future COT. An imagery-only baseline and an otherwise matched historical-COT baseline are fitted on base-training dates; the latter receives eight observed-history COT fields. The historical-COT model is then frozen. Two equal-capacity gated residual heads are fitted on separate correction-training dates: the control has neutral-zero future-COT slots, while the augmented head receives COT from forecast AGRI. Both use the same examples, geometry, sample order and budget. Corrections are signed, with zero-initialized output layers, and the final clear-sky index is nonnegative. Base and correction training use 30 epochs, batch 1,536 and AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine decay to $10^{-5}$). The three random seeds are 42, 43 and 44.
