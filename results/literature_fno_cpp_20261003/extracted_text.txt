Abstract Accurately understanding the evolution and development of cloud physical properties (CPP) in
advance is crucial for extreme weather forecasting and early warning. This study utilized the Fourier neural
operator (FNO) method to develop a short‐term forecasting model of Cloud (Cloud‐FNO). Using the multi‐task
learning framework and autoregression strategy, the model achieves accurate 6‐hr forecasting of cloud phase
(CLP), cloud top height (CTH), cloud effective radius (CER), and cloud optical thickness (COT). Evaluation
results on the independent testing data set show that the Cloud‐FNO model achieves an average CLP
identification accuracy exceeding 74%, and the average root mean square errors for CTH, CER, and COT
forecasts are 2.28 km, 6.52 μm, and 9.01, respectively. Importantly, the Cloud‐FNO model demonstrates strong
forecasting capability and promising application potential for the CPP evolution under severe weather.
Plain Language Summary The development and evolution of clouds are closely related to severe
weather, and the short‐term forecasting of clouds has long been a key topic in the atmospheric sciences. Existing
forecasting methods, such as traditional numerical weather prediction models and those based on statistical or
artificial intelligence, primarily focus on cloud fractions or the cloud movement, with little attention paid to the
future variations in cloud physical properties (CPP). Therefore, this study pioneers the application of the Fourier
neural operator (FNO) method to the field of short‐term forecasting of Cloud, developing a Cloud‐FNO model
to achieve accurate 6‐hr forecasting of CPP. This model fully integrates the advantages of deep learning and the
Fourier transform, capturing the evolutionary trends of CPP comprehensively by combining spatiotemporal
features and frequency‐domain information. On an independent testing data set, the model demonstrated strong
forecasting performance, effectively capturing the development and changes in the CPP of high clouds and thick
clouds. Furthermore, compared with the Simpler yet better Video Prediction model for Cloud forecasting, the 6‐
hr CPP forecasts generated by the Cloud‐FNO model are more consistent with the MODIS official data,
demonstrating the model's stable generalization ability and strong forecasting performance.
1. Introduction
In the Earth‐atmosphere system, the existence and evolution of clouds not only affect the balance of the radiation
budget but also are closely associated with extreme weather processes (Brewster, 2015; Cess et al., 1989; Rossow
& Lacis, 1990; L. Sun et al., 2020). Therefore, acquiring advanced knowledge of cloud development processes is
crucial for predicting and warning of extreme weather events, as well as for enhancing our understanding of future
climate change (Liu et al., 2022; Min et al., 2020).
Cloud forecasting, particularly for short‐term nowcasting (0–6 hr), remains a challenging research topic in the
field of atmospheric science. Typically, numerical weather prediction (NWP) models are employed to simulate
various atmospheric physical variables and weather processes. By implementing cloud microphysical

cannot directly output the cloud physical properties (CPP), such as cloud type, cloud top height (CTH), and cloud
optical thickness (COT). This limitation arises because cloud microphysical processes often occur at small scales,
and the resolution of NWP models may be insufficient to capture these fine‐scale processes accurately (Han
et al., 2015). Furthermore, the selection of different parameterization schemes can significantly influence the
simulation results of the NWP models, and the influence of aerosols on cloud microphysics processes is difficult
to incorporate, which also introduces substantial uncertainty into the prediction of CPP (Huang et al., 2011). In
addition, several studies have adopted traditional statistical methods to predict the development and evolution of
clouds, including linear regression (Das et al., 2009), Fourier transform techniques (Wang et al., 2018), cross‐
correlation (Dissawa et al., 2017; Jamaly & Kleissl, 2018), and optical flow methods (Chow et al., 2015; Sha-
kya & Kumar, 2019), yielding promising preliminary forecasting performance. Nevertheless, these traditional
statistical methods, being grounded in linear theory, are inherently unable to capture the complex nonlinear
transformations within clouds. Worse still, they often suffer from time‐consuming computations and limited
forecasting accuracy.
With the advancement of artificial intelligence technology, deep learning has demonstrated remarkable effec-
tiveness in addressing nonlinear transformation problems, driving a growing shift toward AI‐based methodolo-
gies in cloud forecasting research (Cheng et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2015; Xia et al., 2024). Doshi et al. (2021) utilized
convolutional long short‐term memory (ConvLSTM) networks to predict cloud motion for the next 30 min using
observed satellite cloud images, achieving significant improvements in both forecasting accuracy and compu-
tational efficiency. Similarly, Su et al. (2020) integrated Gated Recurrent Unit, Recurrent Neural Network, and
Convolutional Neural Network architectures to perform short‐term forecasting tasks with infrared cloud images
from the Fengyun‐2 geostationary satellite. This hybrid model outperformed both optical flow and ConvLSTM
models on testing data set. Furthermore, Wei et al. (2023) introduced the CloudpredNet model, which combines a
spatiotemporal attention mechanism—Time‐Space Transformer (Bertasius et al., 2021)—and a Fourier‐
transform‐based time series prediction model—TimesNet (Wu et al., 2022). This model demonstrated stable
performance and capability for accurate short‐term cloud cluster forecasting within 30 min. These studies
highlight the significant potential of deep learning methods in improving the accuracy and efficiency of cloud
forecasting.
However, the aforementioned models for cloud forecasting tend to produce increasingly blurred images as the
forecast time extends. To mitigate this issue, several studies have combined generative adversarial networks with
ConvLSTM modules, introducing a generator and discriminator to improve the quality of the forecasting images
(Lee et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2023). Lu et al. (2021) employed a cascaded ConvLSTM structure for multi‐step
forecasting, followed by training super‐resolution models to refine the results, achieving more detailed and ac-
curate forecasts. Although these approaches partially alleviate the blurring problem, evaluation results indicate
that the generated images may still contain artifacts, introducing additional errors into the forecasts. In recent
years, a Fourier neural operator (FNO) method was proposed to solve parameterized partial differential equations.
The core idea of FNO is to convert the mapping between function spaces into linear transformations in the Fourier
space, which are then parameterized using neural networks. By combining the nonlinear fitting capability of deep
learning with the advantages of the Fourier transform in frequency domain analysis, the FNO method enables
efficient, accurate, and grid‐resolution‐independent solving (Z. Li et al., 2020). It has been widely applied across
various fields, such as rapid flood inundation forecasting (A. Y. Sun et al., 2023) and long‐term turbulence
predictions (Z. Li et al., 2023). Given the success of the FNO method in similar prediction tasks, it holds promise
for achieving improved performance in short‐term cloud forecasting.
Although deep learning methods have demonstrated significant advantages and potential in predicting spatio-
temporal sequences of clouds, existing research has predominantly focused on forecasting cloud fractions and
cloud types, with limited attention given to the prediction of CPP. Moreover, existing research in cloud fore-
casting has typically focused on forecast horizons ranging from 30 min to 4 hr, failing to meet the demand for
longer prediction periods in practical applications (Xia et al., 2024).
Therefore, this study used the FNO method to develop a Cloud forecasting model (Cloud‐FNO) that can realize
short‐term forecasting of cloud macro‐ and micro‐physical properties, including the cloud phase (CLP), CTH,
cloud effective radius (CER), and COT. This model integrates a 3D‐CNN framework with the Fast Fourier
Transform (FFT) module, effectively managing the high‐dimensional spatiotemporal dynamics of CPP data while

2. Data and Methods
2.1. Data
2.1.1. All‐Day CPP Products
Satellite remote sensing technology has emerged as the primary tool for acquiring CPP (Letu et al., 2018; Zhao
et al., 2023). Aqua and Terra polar‐orbiting satellites, equipped with MODIS, operate in sun‐synchronous orbits
approximately 705 km above the surface of Earth, enabling detailed observations of cloud structures with high
spatial resolution. However, MODIS has a longer revisit period and cannot continuously observe specific regions
(Justice et al., 2002). In contrast, geostationary satellites like Himawari‐8 mitigate this limitation to obtain
continuous observation over large coverage regions, despite their higher observational altitude compared to polar‐
orbiting satellites.
To fully leverage the observation advantages of passive imagers on remote sensing satellites, Tong et al. (2023)
and J. Li et al. (2023) have innovatively developed the Cloud‐ResUNet and Cloud‐SmaAtUNet models in the
DaYu‐CLAS, achieving high‐precision and all‐day retrieval of CLP, CTH, CER, and COT by utilizing thermal
infrared observations from Himawari‐8/Advanced Himawari Imager (AHI). These retrieval models not only
integrate the wide observation coverage of geostationary satellites with the high observation accuracy of polar‐
orbiting satellites, but also address the limitation that AHI official products lack nighttime CPP. The all‐day CPP
products retrieved by this method have a temporal resolution of 30 min and a spatial resolution of 5 km, covering a
region from 80° to 200°E and 60°S to 60°N. In this study, we used the all‐day products from March to December
2016 to train the Cloud‐FNO model, while the all‐day products from 2017 were employed for testing and
evaluating the forecasting performance of the Cloud‐FNO model.
2.1.2. MODIS Official Products
The MODIS instrument is mounted on the Terra and Aqua polar‐orbiting satellites, which were launched in 1999
and 2002, respectively. It conducts observations across 36 spectral bands, covering wavelengths ranging from 0.4
to 14.4 μm (King et al., 2013; J. Li et al., 2005), providing cloud products with spatial resolutions of 1 km/5 km
and a temporal resolution of 5 min for different global regions over 20 years (Zhao et al., 2024; Zheng
et al., 2021). In this study, we used the MODIS official products from 2017 as a benchmark to further objectively
compare the forecasting performance of the Cloud‐FNO model with the Cloud‐SimVP model.

2.2. Methods
2.2.1. Data Preprocessing
During the process of data preprocessing, a slicing strategy was adopted to preprocess the all‐day CPP data set
described in Section 2.1.1, with the size of the sample image for each slice set to 512 × 512 pixels (Ronneberger
et al., 2015). These sample images were further organized into time‐series samples for 6‐hr short‐term now-
casting. Since the original data set contained timestamps with retrieval failures, any time series involving such
missing values was removed. After the data preprocessing, a total of 36,148 valid sequences of sample images
were retained. In practical applications, to mitigate artifacts introduced when stitching sample images into a full‐
disk image, a weighted fusion method was used to reconstruct each full‐disk image. Taking into account both the
stitching effect and the computational efficiency, each full‐disk image was required to be sliced with a specified
overlap ratio of 0.5 (Zhao et al., 2024).
To normalize the sample images across different ranges, the following standardization was applied (Equation 1):

Fourier transform, learned spectral filters, inverse transform, and task‐adaptive nonlinear activation functions
(GELU for layers 1–2, ReLU for layers 3–4), making it effectively capture frequency‐domain patterns and
complex spatial features (Z. Li et al., 2020). The Fourier Layers perform FFT to extract spectral features, apply
learned filters in the frequency domain, and reconstruct the signal through inverse FFT. Nonlinear mappings are
then introduced via activation functions. The input is first encoded through a fully connected network and then
symmetrically decoded through two FCNs at the output. Text S1 and Table S1 in Supporting Information S1
provide a more detailed description of frequency‐domain features extracted by the FFT and the application of the
nonlinear activation function.
During the training of the Cloud‐FNO model, we employed the Adam optimizer combined with a learning rate
scheduling strategy to maintain adaptive adjustment throughout the training process, leveraging historical
gradient information to accelerate training (Kingma & Ba, 2014). The model was trained with a total of 300
iterations using a stepwise learning rate (StepLR) scheduler with an initial value of 0.001. Considering training
time, computational resources, and model complexity, we randomly selected 300 sequences from each month of
2016 to form the training data set of the CPP spatiotemporal data set. An additional 1,200 sequences were used for
validation. Furthermore, 1,200 sequences were randomly selected from each month of 2017 to constitute the
testing data set. In practical application, as depicted in Figure 1b, the 0–3 hr CPP forecast is initially generated
from the preceding 3 hr of historical data (−3–0 hr CPP). Subsequently, an autoregressive procedure is applied,
wherein the 0–3 hr forecast is used as the input to derive the 3–6 hr CPP forecast, enabling an extended 6‐hr
forecast horizon.
The network operates in a dual‐branch configuration. The first subnetwork is a classifier dedicated to forecasting
CLP, while the second subnetwork handles multi‐task regression, forecasting CTH, COT, and CER. The multi‐
task framework is based on Sub‐Network Routing with Transformation (SNR‐Trans), where each task consists of
a shared backbone network and task‐specific subnetworks (Ma et al., 2019). Each subnetwork is designed to
handle a specific task and is responsible for learning task‐specific features. For the CLP classification task, we
employ the cross‐entropy loss function to calculate the loss and optimize the model accordingly (Mao
et al., 2023). For the regression tasks of CTH, CER, and COT, considering the equal importance of the three cloud
properties, the Mean Squared Error is adopted as the loss function, and the same loss weight is assigned to
calculate the overall loss and optimize the model.

3. Results and Discussions
3.1. Evaluation on Testing Data Set
To evaluate the performance of the Cloud‐FNO model, an independent testing data set was used to evaluate the
0–6 hr CPP forecasting. Figures 2a–2f show the confusion matrices for CLP identification at different forecast
lead times. The results demonstrate that the overall accuracy (OA) of CLP identification by the Cloud‐FNO model
decreased from 81.92% to 68.07% as the lead time increased. Among all categories, water clouds consistently
achieved the highest classification accuracy, while clear sky exhibited the lowest at each forecast step. Despite
this decline, the average OA over the 0–6 hr period remained above 74%, indicating that the Cloud‐FNO model
retains a strong forecasting capability for accurate CLP identification.
The joint probability density distributions of CTH, CER, and COT at different forecast lead times are shown in
Figures 2g–2x. The high‐density regions (darker colors) between both forecasted and true values are predomi-
nantly concentrated near the diagonal line, suggesting strong forecasting skill of the Cloud‐FNO model. As the
lead time increases, the model's forecasting performance gradually declines, with the RMSE values for CTH,
CER, and COT increasing from 1.63 to 2.81 km, 4.84–7.80 μm, and 6.75 to 10.54, respectively, while the R values
decrease from 0.93 to 0.78, 0.85 to 0.61, and 0.84 to 0.56, respectively. Nevertheless, the Cloud‐FNO model
maintains reasonable forecast performance throughout the 6‐hr period, with the average RMSE (MAE) values for
CTH, CER, and COT being 2.28 km (1.54 km), 6.52 μm (4.82 μm), and 9.01 (5.57), respectively. Furthermore,
the average PSNR values for CTH, CER, and COT were 18.08 dB, 17.51 dB, and 22.43 dB, respectively, and the
corresponding SSIM values were 0.62, 0.60, and 0.63. These outcomes clearly demonstrate the robust perfor-
mance of the Cloud‐FNO model in short‐term forecasting of CPP.
3.2. Evaluation on MODIS Data
To further objectively evaluate the generalization capability and forecasting performance of the Cloud‐FNO
model, we assessed the precision of the 6‐hr CPP forecasted by the Cloud‐FNO and Cloud‐SimVP models, as
well as compared it with the precision of true all‐day products, using the MODIS official products as the
benchmark. The SimVP model excels in video time series prediction for its streamlined architecture, high
computational efficiency, and outstanding predictive performance (Gao et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2025), which has
also demonstrated robust performance in 6‐hr radar image and precipitation nowcasting tasks (Pyo et al., 2025).
As shown in Table 1, although the average OA of the 6‐hr CLP identification forecasted by the Cloud‐FNO model
was about 10% lower than that from the true all‐day CPP products, it still reaches 69.18%, which is significantly
higher than the 43.86% achieved by the Cloud‐SimVP model. Even the improved Cloud‐SimVP integrated with

It is worth noting that mixed‐phase clouds were excluded from the CLP
predictions in this study. According to the statistics derived from the MODIS
official cloud products with a 30‐min temporal resolution in January 2017, the
occurrence frequency of all clouds was 63.44%, while that of mixed‐phase
clouds was only 2.0%. Given the OA of 80.26% achieved by the true all‐
day CPP products, the potential influence of mixed‐phase clouds on predic-
tion accuracy is around 2%, which is considered negligible.

Specifically, the Cloud‐FNO model accurately forecasts the movement and changes of ice clouds, high clouds with
CTH >10 km, larger‐particle clouds with CER >30 μm, and thick clouds with COT >10 in the equatorial region
(around 10°S) and mid‐high latitudes (around 40°N and 40°S). These results indicate that the Cloud‐FNO model
exhibits considerable capability and skill in forecasting rapid CPP changes under extreme weather conditions.
4. Conclusions
In this study, a Cloud‐FNO model was proposed for short‐term forecasting of CPP within a 6‐hr timeframe,
representing the first‐time application of the FNO method in the field of cloud forecasting. The Cloud‐FNO model
effectively integrates the strengths of deep learning and Fourier transforms, incorporating both spatiotemporal
features and frequency‐domain information, thereby enabling a more comprehensive capture of CPP evolution
trends. Additionally, a multi‐task learning framework is adopted to facilitate simultaneous training and parameter
optimization across multiple variables, and an autoregressive strategy is employed to achieve optimal general-
ization ability and forecasting performance within the 6‐hr forecasting window.
The evaluation findings based on the testing data set validate the robustness and reliability of the Cloud‐FNO
model. For 6‐hr forecasts, the average OA of CLP identification reaches over 74%. The RMSE values for
CTH, CER, and COT forecasting are 2.28 km, 6.52 μm, and 9.01, respectively. Validation based on independent
third‐party MODIS official products shows that the forecast precision of the Cloud‐FNO model is superior to that
of other AI‐based prediction models, and is comparable to that of true all‐day CPP products, further confirming
the reliability of its forecasting performance objectively. Moreover, the model demonstrates superior forecasting
capability for the movement and changes of cloud systems such as high clouds and thick clouds, particularly
under severe convective weather conditions.
Despite these encouraging results, several aspects merit further improvement. Preserving image structural in-
formation and reducing image blur as lead time increases remain significant challenges in spatiotemporal fore-
casting. Future work could focus on incorporating additional atmospheric variables to enhance the accuracy of
CPP short‐term forecasting. Considering the inherent uncertainties in cloud forecasting, ensemble forecasting
techniques could provide a more comprehensive assessment of CPP variations under different weather scenarios
and offer valuable probabilistic insights.
